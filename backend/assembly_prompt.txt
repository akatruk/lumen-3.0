EDITORIAL OBJECTIVE

Produce a coherent, carefully composed vertical explainer.

The original presenter remains the protagonist. Preserve the speaker’s identity, authentic meaning, factual claims, and intended message.

Visuals must explain what is being said at that moment. Do not turn the speech into a sequence of disconnected stock images or decorative title cards.

Do not invent statistics, legal conclusions, promises, quotations, or a call to action absent from the source.

Use the supplied final speech audio. Do not generate another presenter, fabricate lip-sync, or substitute a different voice or language.

PLAN THE WHOLE VIDEO BEFORE COMPOSING

Read the entire aligned transcript and organize it into complete semantic beats.

For each beat, choose:
- the thought the viewer should understand;
- presenter framing;
- an appropriate visual explanation;
- relevant supplied assets;
- readable text;
- entrance, emphasis, and exit motion.

A semantic beat may span several subtitle phrases. Do not create a new graphic card for each fragment of speech.

Build an opening, progression, and conclusion from the actual source. Do not force a generic marketing narrative onto educational speech.

GRAPHICS COVERAGE THROUGHOUT THE VIDEO

Apply the requested graphics coverage to the entire duration.

Distribute graphic scenes across the beginning, middle, and final third. Do not spend the entire graphics allowance in the first half.

At 100% graphics coverage, every spoken beat receives a meaningful graphic treatment with the presenter visibly integrated.

At lower coverage, select the beats that benefit most from visual explanation and use full-frame presenter footage elsewhere.

Do not insert irrelevant media to meet a percentage. Where no supplied asset fits, use a clear diagram or typographic explanation grounded in the speech.

Do not create a long, unexplained static presenter section after the graphics have ended. Allow longer presenter shots when the content warrants them.

PRESENTER SIZE AND PRESENCE

During graphic explanations, show the original presenter in a clearly visible circle or rounded PIP window.

For a vertical frame, begin with a presenter-window width of approximately 28–35% of the frame width. Increase it when needed to keep facial expressions readable at phone size.

The presenter must never become a tiny decorative avatar.

Frame the face and shoulders naturally. Preserve facial detail, avoid excessive cropping, and keep the footage sharp.

Use a small number of consistent presenter positions. Move the presenter only when the new composition requires it.

Outside graphic explanations, use the original presenter full-frame with restrained, purposeful reframing.

FACE PROTECTION — REQUIRED

Reserve separate areas for:
1. the presenter;
2. the main graphic explanation;
3. subtitles.

No title, card, caption, caption backing, connector, or decorative object may cross the presenter’s face.

Apply this rule throughout entrance, hold, emphasis, and exit animations, including overshoot.

A translucent overlay still counts as an obstruction.

When a collision occurs:
- reposition the graphic or presenter window;
- change the composition;
- shorten the graphic text;
- remove a secondary element.

Do not solve collisions by making the presenter tiny or the subtitles unreadable.

CAPTIONS — PHONE READABILITY

Render captions in the target language, synchronized to the supplied target-language speech timing.

Use large, strong typography with a contrasting backing. For 1080×1920 output, start around 56–72 pixels and adjust for the actual font and language while preserving phone readability.

Use one or two lines. Keep comfortable side margins and clearance from platform controls.

Split long sentences into short, meaningful timed phrases. Never squeeze a sentence into one tiny line or shrink text until it becomes unreadable.

Keep words intact in English and Russian. Use appropriate Chinese phrase boundaries and punctuation.

Keep the caption block stable during word highlighting. Do not make the line jump or resize as each word becomes active.

Avoid letter-by-letter subtitle reveals that briefly display an isolated letter. Display readable phrases; use restrained keyword emphasis if appropriate.

Position captions in their reserved area without covering the presenter’s face or the main information.

GRAPHIC TEXT IS NOT A COPY OF EVERY SUBTITLE

Graphic headings express complete ideas. Subtitles reproduce speech.

Never use fragments such as “and the”, “but this”, or “usually” as standalone information cards.

A heading should communicate the concept being explained, using concise wording in the target language.

Do not duplicate the same sentence in a title, card, and subtitle simultaneously.

FINISHED GRAPHIC SCENES

Compose each graphic scene around one dominant idea.

Use substantial, readable elements with clear hierarchy. Avoid a huge empty panel containing a tiny sentence.

Choose the visual form from the meaning:
- comparison: two clearly labeled alternatives;
- ownership: participants and labeled shares;
- process: steps appearing in order;
- money movement: a labeled directional flow;
- stated percentage: a large number with its actual explanation;
- timeline: real events in their stated order;
- location or property: a relevant supplied image with a concise contextual label.

Use arrows only when the speech describes a direction, relationship, flow, rise, or fall.

Do not animate counters toward invented figures. Do not imply growth when the source discusses ownership or control.

Let elements reveal in a meaningful sequence: establish the idea, introduce supporting information, emphasize the conclusion, then transition.

Allow enough time to read and understand. Avoid arbitrary flashes and constant motion.

USE THE SUPPLIED MEDIA LIBRARY

Use the selected library assets where they support the current spoken idea.

Consider relevant assets throughout the entire video, including the final third. Do not confine library use to the opening.

Use supplied photographs, approved motion clips, and objects as parts of composed scenes:
- an explanatory background;
- a contextual image;
- a foreground object;
- a supporting motion layer;
- a short relevant cutaway.

Preserve the presenter’s prominence during graphic treatments.

Do not repeat a generic meeting, building, passport, or globe merely because it is available. Prefer semantic relevance over visual decoration.

Respect asset aspect ratios, quality, transparency, and safe crop areas. Do not stretch images.

If an asset is unavailable or unsuitable, omit it and use a source-grounded graphic explanation. Never substitute unrelated imagery.

DESIGN AND MOTION

Use one coherent visual system with:
- readable typography;
- strong contrast;
- deliberate spacing;
- a restrained palette;
- consistent card and presenter-window treatment.

Avoid thin decorative frames surrounding mostly empty space.

Motion should explain relationships and guide attention. Use purposeful reveals, connected diagrams, comparisons, and restrained media movement.

Vary compositions when the content changes. Do not repeat the same card layout through three consecutive semantic beats.

Do not add random effects merely to increase visual variety.

AUDIO

Follow the supplied voice and music settings.

When source and target language match, preserve the original speech unless the user explicitly selected a replacement voice.

When they differ, use the supplied target-language audio and alignment. Do not reuse source-language subtitle timing.

If music is enabled and a supplied approved track is available, mix it below speech with gentle ducking and smooth fades.

If music is disabled, do not add it.

Use subtle sound accents only where they improve a meaningful reveal. Avoid repeated whooshes.

Maintain intelligible speech, continuous audio, and freedom from clipping.

FINAL REVIEW BEFORE DELIVERY

Review the entire rendered video, including transitions and the final third.

Correct any occurrence of:
- face obstruction;
- tiny presenter window;
- unreadable or clipped subtitles;
- incomplete graphic headings;
- unrelated library imagery;
- empty oversized cards;
- graphics concentrated only in the opening;
- incorrect language;
- invented claims;
- poor speech synchronization;
- music overpowering speech.

The final result must be a complete, readable visual explanation of the original speech, with a recognizable presenter and relevant supplied media throughout the requested graphics coverage.
